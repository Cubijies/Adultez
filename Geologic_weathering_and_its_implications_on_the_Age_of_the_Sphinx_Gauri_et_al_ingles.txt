The Great Sphinx of Giza is considered by Egyptologists to have been excavated by the 
Pharaoh Kephren nearly 4500 years ago. Schoch and West zyxw

the Sphinx is much older, based primarily upon the rounded profile of the strata of the

INTRODUCTION 
At the 1992 Annual Meeting in Chicago, the American Association for the 
Advancement of Science (AAAS) featured a debate on the subject of the age 
of the Great Sphinx of Giza, Egypt (Culotta, 1992). This debate was motivated 
by several articles in the press based upon the work of Robert M. Schoch, 
Professor of Science and Mathematics at Boston University’s College of Basic 
Studies, and John A. West, an amateur Egyptologist who has suggested for 
many years that an advanced civilization must have existed at Giza before the 
time of the Pharaohs who built the pyramids ca 2600 B.C. In their account, 
the Sphinx is a product of that unrecorded civilization. 
The Egyptologic evidence for an age of nearly 4500 years for the Sphinx 
derives in part from the theory that Pharaoh Kephren created the Sphinx 
as suggested by the context of its setting among other important pharaonic 
monuments (Figure 1). Kephren’s pyramid, the site of his burial, is located 
just west of the Sphinx. The Valley Temple, where he was anointed before 
burial, is situated immediately to the southeast, perhaps at the former bank 
of the Nile River. The causeway, the route of Kephren’s funerary procession, 
skirts the south ditch of the Sphinx. Additionally, some Egyptologists believe 
that the face of the Sphinx resembles that of Kephren, though this idea has 
been challenged by West (1992) on the basis of recent forensic evidence. Since 
no artifacts dating prior to 2600 B.C. have been found in the area of the Sphinx

GEOLOGICAL FEATURES OF THE SOUTHEASTERN GIZA 
PLATEAU AND THE SPHINX 
To fully understand the arguments presented, one first needs to have an 
overview of the geology of the area. The southeastern Giza Plateau is made of 
the upper portion of the Eocene Mokkatam Formation, which consists of lime- 
stone layers from which the Sphinx was carved (Figure 2). One of the critical 
features of the limestone is its porosity, a feature controlled in limestone largely 
by diagenetic events. The evolution of porosity in Sphinx limestone is described 
by Chowdhury et al. (1990). 
The limestone strata exposed at the Sphinx can be divided into three litho- 
logic units (Gauri, 1984) as shown in Figures 3a and 3b and summarized below. 
The lowest unit is a reefal limestone interbedded with soft limestone layers. 
The middle unit, which is crucial to the present study, is made of a cyclothem 
of seven layers of biomicrite limestone, i.e., a fine grained limestone with 
discrete skeletal particles. The lower portion of each layer of this unit is a 
somewhat softer limestone and has larger amounts of water-soluble salts (halite 
and gypsum) and detrital material (clay minerals and quartz dust) than its 
upper portion. In the ascending sequence, the salt and the detrital material, 
in general, decrease so that the seventh layer is a clean, white, hard limestone.

The upper unit is a similar cyclothem, but consists of only two layers. How-

Eocene tectonism (Omara, 1952:66; Gauri, 1984) produced a dip of the strata 
in a southeasterly direction with a slope of 2.5-5". Thus, the surface of the 
Giza plateau, north and northwest of the Sphinx, is comprised of the lowest 
lithologic unit, as is the base of the Khufu pyramid (Figure 2). On the northern 
portion of the Sphinx body this unit is covered by the veneer stone of several 
restorations. In the southeast corner of the Sphinx, in the ditch, this unit dips 
into the subsurface under the middle unit (Figures 3a,b). 
The torso of the Sphinx body is formed from the middle lithologic unit, with 
all seven layers exposed. In the wall along the south ditch, only the four lower 
layers are exposed (Figure 3b). South of the Causeway, the three upper layers, 
poorly exposed due to erosion and the sand cover, can be seen in the immediate 
vicinity of the Valley Temple. Farther south, these and other older beds are 
buried under sand (Figure 4). 
The neck, face, and head areas of the Sphinx are formed from the upper unit 
only. The two layers of the upper unit can be traced to the southeast overlying 
the middle unit just west of the Valley Temple. Farther west, only layers 
higher than those at the head of the Sphinx are exposed, all the units of the 
Sphinx being in the subsurface (Figure 4). These higher layers are again 
alternating hard and soft beds, but unlike the layers of the middle unit, some 
of them mark a relatively sharper change in lithology. 
The structural deformation that produced the dip described above also cre- 
ated two sets of intersecting, nearly vertical, irregularly spaced joints (Omara, 
1952:62; Gauri, 1984). These joints, or fractures, are well developed in the 
more rigid strata and feather out in the softer layers. Thus, the lowest unit 
and the upper parts of the layers in the middle unit reveal well-developed 
fractures.

when the Sphinx was carved into the country rock. However, most of these 
cavities at the Sphinx were covered over by the masonry construction. Never- 
theless, one such major channel can be seen passing through the posterior part 
of the Sphinx (Figure 3a) and continuing in the ditch wall to the south of the 
Sphinx. Similar channels can also be seen in the shafts of deep burial sites in

THE WEATHERING PROCESS 
The present-day weathering of the Sphinx is primarily due to pressure gener- 
ated in the pores of the limestone by salt crystallization (Gauri, 1984, 1990; 
Yerrapragada et al., 1993). Often one can see chips of stone peeling from the 
rock mass soon after sunrise. The lower surfaces of these chips are coated with 
halite and gypsum (Gauri, 1984). The indicated mechanism of this exfoliation

is that in the cool of the night, with the arrival of the dew point, water vapor 
condenses on the stone surface. The water is then drawn into the pores by 
capillary suction. The salt, present at the surface as well as lodged in the pores, 
goes into solution. As the sun rises and the water begins to evaporate, incipient 
crystals of salt begin to form in the solution in the pores. These crystals generate 
hydrostatic pressure directed against the pore walls, which disrupt when the 
pressure exceeds the strength of the rock. 
This mode of stone disintegration, although also seen in humid climates, is 
most common in desert areas (Wellman and Wilson, 1965; Cooke, 1981). First 
of all, the small amount of water formed by condensation produces a highly 
concentrated solution from which the salt can easily crystallize as the evapora- 
tion begins. Second, the salts are not leached away due to the general scarcity 
of water; this is why the desert soils are so rich in salt. Third, the extreme 
climate, which causes a high degree of cooling of rock in the night, enables 
the moisture to condense no matter how scarce the moisture. Fourth, salts like 
halite, are hygroscopic and are able to condense water at a moisture level that 
otherwise may be quite inadequate for dew to form. 
In addition to the salt content, the pore-size distributions influence the dura- 
bility of rock to weathering (Gauri, 1990). It is known that when small pores, 
say less than 1 pm in diameter, are abundant in a rock, this rock will be much 
less durable than a similar rock with many large pores (Chowdhury et al., 
1990; Yerrapragada et al., 1993). This weathering process gives rise to the 
vertical profile of the Sphinx and the walls of the Sphinx enclosure made 
of alternating projections and recessions. This profile is rolling because the 
transition of lithology and petrofabrics from one layer to the next is rather 
gradual. 
The structural joints in the rock have also significantly contributed to weath- 
ering. Because of greater weathering along the joints than in the space between 
them, the internal pressure due to salt crystallization has produced what may 
be called spheroidal weathering. zyxwv
As a result, rounded surfaces have formed 
both in the lateral as well as in vertical direction (Figure zyx
5). This process is 
quite active today as seen from thick debris consisting of salt and limestone 
powder and flakes lying at the foot of the walls where these rounded structures 
have formed. Additionally, in the softer rock underneath these rounded forms, 
where the joints feather out, deep hollows have formed accompanied again 
with large amounts of recent debris. 
WEATHERING FEATURES AND THE AGE OF THE SPHINX 
Radiocarbon dating, commonly used to determine the absolute age of archaeo- 
logical objects, cannot be applied to the Sphinx because the masonry associated 
with the Sphinx lacks any carbon-bearing materials, nor have any other meth- 
ods been found to determine its absolute age. However, Schoch (1992a, 199213, 
1992c, 19928 has proposed an age of 7000-9000 years on the basis of the 
degree and the type of weathering.

Schoch has recognized two types of profiles developed in the middle unit at 
the southeast Giza plateau. According to him, one of these profiles, exposed at 
the Sphinx, is termed rolling profile and the other, exposed at the Giza necropo- 
lis, the angular profile. Schoch has attributed these profiles to “precipitation- 
induced weathering’’ and “wind-induced weathering,” respectively. In his con- 
ception, “precipitation-induced weathering” is the reason that the projections 
and reentrants are rounded while these are angular in the case of “wind- 
induced weathering.” 
Whatever the precise mechanisms behind “precipitation-induced” and “wind- 
induced” weathering may be, these terms seem to be fundamentally flawed. 
Weathering, as commonly understood, depends upon the intrinsic properties 
of rock. In the context of the Sphinx these properties are the pore-size distribu- 
tions and the salt content of the pores. These properties, aided by atmospheric 
moisture, facilitate weathering and not erosion caused by wind or rain. We will 
discuss below these profiles and other features that Schoch cites as indicators of 
Sphinx origin as being early Holocene pluvial and show that none of these 
features can be used to give an absolute age to the Sphinx.

Further, Schoch considers a two-stage excavation of the Sphinx. He argues 
that the frontal portion had been excavated in prepharaonic time and the rear 
portion later, by the Pharaoh Khafre (Schoch, 1992a). One can conclude from 
this, following Schoch’s theory, that the vertical profile of the wall behind the 
rear of the Sphinx ditch should be angular. But this is not the case. In fact, 
the vertical profiles of the strata of the Sphinx on all four sides as well as those 
of the south and west walls of the enclosure are rounded and exactly similar.

of the Sphinx enclosure having been formed by the rain and running water (see 
photograph captioned “Ditch Wall of the Sphinx enclosure showing weathering 
features” on the back of Schoch [1992a]). As we have shown above, these 
cavities are of pre-Pliocene age, having formed by the underground water and 
are not a result of erosion since the Sphinx had been excavated and the ditch 
formed. However, rainfall, even in the modern desert environment, is a potent 
agent of erosion and may have contributed to the widening of the preexisting 
channels.

Deep Weathering of the Sphinx and the Valley Temple 
in Pharaonic Times zyxwv
A large scale restoration of the Sphinx is believed to have been carried out 
in pharaonic times (Hawass, 1992). The weathered profile preserved under the 
restoration blocks shows that the Sphinx had already weathered deeply at that 
time. Also, limestone blocks, presumably excavated from around the frontal 
part of the Sphinx, were used to build the Valley Temple. These blocks were 
covered later, in pharaonic time, by granite blocks, because the limestone had 
weathered so intensely. Schoch argues that a long period of time must have 
elapsed between this restoration and the creation of the Sphinx in order to 
account for this deep weathering. 
A large amount of debris due to weathering at the base of the walls around 
the Sphinx has accumulated where, at the end of Braize restoration about 
1926, a clean surface had been produced. This is despite frequent cleaning in 
recent times. Further, Figure 8 shows a limestone block lying at the ditch of 
the Sphinx intended to be used in a recent restoration. Within one season 
considerable exfoliation had occurred, the edges had worn away, and a rounded 
weathered surface had formed. Thus, it is clear that long periods of time are 
not necessary for the deep weathering of certain rocks to occur.

Larger Pores in Upper Layers of the Sphinx Thorax 
Referring to our studies (Gauri et al., 1990) on pore-size distributions and 
noting that large pores are more abundant in higher strata of the Sphinx 
thorax, Schoch (1992a) states, zyxwv
“As rain fell on the back of the Sphinx (or at

just rain during the early Holocene that is only a few thousand years old. 
Subsurface Weathering of the Sphinx Ditch 
A seismic survey of the subsurface within the Sphinx Enclosure (Dobecki, 
1992; Dobecki and Schoch, 1992) along north-south and east-west lines in 
the ditch reveals that the depth of “weathering” in the posterior portion of the 
Sphinx is only 1.2 m and it increases abruptly to 1.8-2.5 m in the remaining 
portion of the Enclosure. The authors conclude from this that the rear of the 
Sphinx was excavated later than the front in order to prove their theory that 
monuments in the necropolis posterior to the Sphinx are of pharanoic origin 
and the rest of the Sphinx predates the pharaonic times. 
This feature of the variable depth of “weathering” and, particularly, the 
abrupt change in this depth in the posterior portion of the Sphinx, though 
challenged as being artifact or due to inhomogeneity of rock (Harrell, 19941, 
could be a significant argument in favor of two-stage excavation of the Sphinx. 
But this feature alone cannot be used to give an age to the Sphinx.
